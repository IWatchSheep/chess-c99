## 1. How I understand your new idea

Your existing chess program does this:

1. Select a piece.
2. Display its legal destinations.
3. Select a destination and imagine the move without actually playing it.
4. Show how that move changes the available destinations of the other pieces.

Your new statement adds another operation.

For every available movement, the calculator asks a TRUE/FALSE question. Depending on the answer, it can examine the next available movements and ask another TRUE/FALSE question, continuing until it reaches the loop limit.

That gives us this conceptual flow:

Select piece

List possible movements

Imagine one movement

Does the motion satisfy the stated condition?

TRUE

Record the condition that was satisfied

FALSE

Record the condition that was not satisfied

Continue exploring available movements within the limit

One crucial point: FALSE does not necessarily mean that a move is bad. It only means that the particular condition being tested was not satisfied. It may still lead to a useful result through another movement.

Likewise, TRUE means a condition was satisfied, not that a complete chess strategy has been proven.

This preserves the experimental approach you've wanted from the beginning.

## 2. Answers to Claude's four questions

### Question 1 — Is Claude's interpretation faithful?

Yes, with one clarification.

Claude proposes interpreting the TRUE/FALSE result as whether a particular goal has been reached.

That is a reasonable first test of your idea, but your statement is broader.

For example, a movement might be tested for whether it reaches a square, creates protection, restores a previously lost movement, or enables a subsequent movement.

I recommend defining the underlying rule as:

\\[ T(m,C)= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if movement }m\text{ satisfies condition }C\\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\]

Here, \\(C\\) is an explicitly defined condition, not necessarily the goal itself.

In Version 11, \\(C\\) can initially mean reaching a specified square. Later experiments can test other conditions using the same structure.

This is my mathematical formalization of your statement, not a formula you originally wrote.

### Question 2 — Marks per piece or for the whole side?

I recommend both, but kept separate.

Suppose White wants to reach f7.

| Question                           | Example result                                                    |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Can this selected bishop reach f7? | TRUE                                                              |
| Can this selected queen reach f7?  | TRUE within two own moves, under suitable hypothetical conditions |
| Can some White piece reach f7?     | TRUE                                                              |
| Is the arriving piece protected?   | Depends on the resulting position                                 |

These are different facts. Combining them into a single TRUE/FALSE result would hide information that your calculator is supposed to reveal.

The program should distinguish `PIECE TRUE/FALSE` from `SIDE TRUE/FALSE`, with the stated condition and depth beside each.

### Question 4 — What tests should be added?

Claude's proposed tests are a good starting point. I would add checks for the distinction between reaching a square and being protected there; a FALSE result that becomes TRUE after a later move; goal sets where several squares are TRUE but only the first is selected by the `if / else if` ordering; and movements that cease to be legal.

Also, PASS mode needs a precise definition. Ignoring the opponent's turns is useful for measuring hypothetical movement distance, but it must not allow a piece to teleport, jump through blockers, or ignore its own king's safety. The output must clearly identify PASS as a hypothetical calculation, not a legal sequence of alternating chess turns.
